# M4: The Engine

> Swap flutter_soloud for the Lira Rust engine over FFI behind the existing
> `AudioEngine` interface. A and B test latency and battery before and after.
> If soloud is not the bottleneck, defer indefinitely.
>
> GATE: measurable improvement users would notice. If the honest answer is "it
> is just cleaner architecture," do not merge.

Everything a desk can do is landed: the mixer exists in Rust, renders the same
samples as the Dart one, runs inside a real audio callback behind the existing
`AudioEngine` interface, converts what it is fed without folding aliases into
it, and gives the output back when the phone asks for it. `SoLoudAudioEngine`
is still what ships by default and stays that way until stage 3 answers the
gate with numbers taken on a phone. That order is deliberate, and the first
number worth having was what the current engine costs.

## The measurement that changes the plan

`tool/engine_bench.dart` renders a minute of a four card song, both machines,
every step modifier, swing, a busy sub lane, eight kit slots, in 1024 frame
blocks. Best of three after a warm up, on an Apple M4 Pro, macOS 26.5:

| mixer | per 1024 frame block | of one core |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dart, JIT (`dart run`) | 65.0 us | 0.28% |
| Dart, AOT (`dart compile exe`) | 33.6 us | 0.14% |
| Rust, release | 21.0 us | 0.09% |

AOT Dart is 1.6x slower than Rust. On a desktop core that is a rounding error,
and even twenty times slower on a cold phone it is a few percent of one core.

**So the case for M4 is not CPU, and it is not battery.** Anyone arguing the
Rust engine on throughput is arguing about a percent and a half of a core. If
that were the whole case, the gate would say no and mean it.

The case is latency, and it is structural rather than a matter of speed:

- `SoLoudAudioEngine` keeps 0.24 s of rendered audio queued ahead of the
  playhead (`_queueSeconds`), and tops it up from a 12 ms Dart timer. It has to.
  The Dart mixer runs on the event loop, and the event loop is not allowed
  anywhere near an audio callback: a GC pause or a rebuilding widget tree would
  be a dropout.
- That quarter second is the worst case delay before a painted step is audible.
  It is also the accuracy floor of the playhead, which is read back from block
  markers against the frames the device says it has consumed.
- A Rust mixer runs **inside** the callback. An edit lands at the next block
  boundary: about 12 ms at 512 frames, 23 ms at 1024. The playhead is then the
  callback's own frame counter rather than an estimate.

Paint a step and hear it now, instead of up to a quarter second later, on an
instrument whose entire loop is painting steps and listening. That is the
improvement a user would notice, and it is the one thing the Dart mixer cannot
be optimised into.

## What Lira actually gives us

Lira is at `~/Projects/lira`, and it is a desktop DAW. Being honest about what
converges:

- **The shape of `lira-graph::controller` is the thing to copy.** One control
  thread owns the cpal stream. Control to audio is lock free: POD commands over
  an SPSC ring, whole plans published through their own queue with a return
  path so that nothing is ever deallocated on the audio thread. junglEngine
  needs a much smaller version of exactly that.
- **`lira-dsp` and `lira-pitch` are reusable primitives** — SVF and ladder
  filters, ADSR, shaper, resampler. junglEngine's sub synth is one Chamberlin
  SVF and a rational tanh and it already has both, so this is a later tidy up,
  not a dependency to take on now.
- **`lira-graph` itself does not come over.** It hosts VST3 plugins, takes MIDI
  input, captures and monitors inputs, and fans rendering out across a worker
  pool. junglEngine is one stereo output, four slice voices, sixteen kit voices
  and one sub. Taking the graph would be taking a DAW into a phone app, which
  is the thing CLAUDE.md exists to prevent.
- **Lira has never been built for a phone.** Its app targets macOS, Windows and
  Linux. cpal has iOS and Android backends, but nothing in Lira exercises them,
  so that risk belongs to junglEngine and gets retired in stage 2, early.

So "converge on Lira" here means converging on its architecture, and on its DSP
crates when there is a second caller for them. It does not mean linking its
workspace into a phone app.

## Stage 1: the mixer, in Rust (done)

`packages/junglengine_engine/rust`, and it is deliberately only the mixer.

- `spec.rs` reads the app's own JSON. A `Beat` there parses exactly what
  `Beat.toJson()` writes here, defaults and all, so the FFI wire format, the
  saved project format and the parity fixtures are one format. Specs cross the
  boundary at human speed, never in the callback.
- `sub_voice.rs` and `renderer.rs` are ports, coefficient for coefficient, of
  `lib/audio/sub_voice.dart` and `lib/audio/pattern_renderer.dart`. Nothing
  below the block loop allocates.
- `test/audio/rust_parity_test.dart` renders four fixtures through both mixers
  and compares them frame by frame: chop with every step modifier and swing,
  kit with velocities and slot tuning, a song running both machines, and the
  sub lane alone. Nothing is committed as a golden; the Dart side writes the
  source audio it rendered with and the Rust binary reads those bytes, so what
  is compared is the two mixers rather than two noise generators agreeing.

Today the two agree **exactly**, sample for sample, on all four fixtures. The
tolerance is 5e-6 anyway, because `sin`, `exp` and `pow` are not obliged to
give Dart and Rust the same last bit on every platform and a resonant filter
carries a last bit difference forward.

The test is skipped, loudly, when there is no cargo on the machine: `flutter
test` still has to pass on a checkout that has never built the engine.

## Stage 2: the device (done, except on a phone)

In order, because each step can fail on its own and should fail early.

### 1. The phone backends

`cpal` builds and links for `aarch64-apple-ios`, `aarch64-apple-ios-sim`,
`aarch64-linux-android`, `armv7-linux-androideabi` and `x86_64-linux-android`.
Two things came out of it that were worth finding before the FFI was written:

- **Android needs no JavaVM.** cpal's Android backend is AAudio through the
  `ndk` crate, and it reaches for `ndk_context` -- the JavaVM and the Activity
  -- only to *enumerate* devices. `default_output_device()` returns a device
  without asking, and the default output is the only one a phone app wants. So
  there is no `JNI_OnLoad`, no Kotlin shim and no `ndk-context` wiring.
- **Android needs API 26.** AAudio arrives in Android 8.0, and
  `libaaudio.so` is the one `DT_NEEDED` in the built object that the app's
  current minimum of 24 does not have. The plugin's `minSdk` is deliberately
  left at 24 so that depending on it does not silently raise the whole app's
  minimum: below 26 the shared object simply fails to load, which is a case
  the fallback in stage 3's list has to cover. If the A/B says the Rust engine
  ships, the plugin and the app go to 26 together.

`cargo run --bin je_device` opens the real default output and plays a bar
through the whole stack -- control thread, plan ring, trash ring, renderer,
device -- rather than a sine through cpal alone, which is a good deal more than
a sine proves. **On a phone this is still Brian's to run**: the app itself is
the test now, with `--dart-define=JE_LIRA_ENGINE=true`, and that is the top of
stage 3's list.

### 2. The control thread and the plan swap

`device.rs`, and it is `lira_graph::controller` with everything a phone does
not have taken out: no device picker, no input capture, no MIDI, no worker
pool. One thread owns the `cpal::Stream` for its whole life; every call from
Dart arrives as a message rather than touching the stream.

The rule the rest of the crate exists to keep is that the callback does not
allocate, deallocate, lock or block:

- **Plans arrive built.** `Timeline` moved out of the renderer into `plan.rs`,
  so every `Vec` of step boundaries is allocated on the control thread. A spec
  change used to rebuild them inside the mixer, which was fine when the mixer
  ran ahead of the callback and is not fine now.
- **Plans leave whole.** Every method that displaces a plan returns it
  `#[must_use]`, and the one place a plan retires from inside `render` -- a
  queued Beat landing on a bar line -- parks it in a slot the callback drains.
  All of it goes back over the trash ring to be dropped on the control thread.
- **Both directions are `rtrb`**, the SPSC ring Lira's controller uses. The
  trash ring is sized past everything that feeds it so the callback's push can
  never fail.

`SpecChange.nextBar` came over with it: the queued swap the Dart renderer does
on a bar line now happens in Rust, so switching Beats mid bar still finishes
the bar.

### 3. The C ABI

`ffi.rs`, and it is `AudioEngine` and nothing more: eighteen entry points, one
per method on the interface plus the shared transport and the last error --
twenty once stage 5 added a method for the output going away and one for it
coming back, which is the rule working rather than an exception to it. A
spec is the app's own JSON, parsed on the caller's thread. `RenderSpec` grew a
`toEngineJson`, so the file format, the FFI boundary and the parity fixtures
are one format written in one place.

Two things are deliberately *not* calls:

- **The playhead.** `je_engine_transport` hands back a pointer to a `#[repr(C)]`
  struct of atomics that the callback writes at the end of every block and Dart
  reads once per frame. No call per frame, and no estimate: the position is the
  frame that is sounding. The one subtlety is that a Beat id is a `String` and
  the callback may not touch one, so a section index and a plan id cross
  instead and Dart resolves the id against the plan the position came from --
  which during a queued swap is not the newest spec it sent.
- **The audio.** A break is seconds of samples and an edit publishes a spec at
  pointer rate, so clips cross when they change and a published plan takes an
  `Arc` of what is already there. That is also what makes an edit adoptable:
  the renderer's test for "this can go in under the playhead" is that the new
  plan reads the same buffer.

`renderOffline` is in the ABI from the start, as promised, and
`test/audio/rust_parity_test.dart` now renders every fixture through the export
path as well as the playback one. Both agree with the Dart mixer sample for
sample.

### 4. The Flutter FFI plugin

`packages/junglengine_engine` is now a plugin. iOS gets an `.xcframework`
(a device slice and a simulator slice are different platforms, so they cannot
share one archive), macOS a fat static library, Android one `.so` per ABI built
with the NDK's own clang wrappers -- no `cargo-ndk`, so the toolchain
requirement is rustup and nothing else.

Verified end to end: `flutter build ios`, `flutter build macos` and `flutter
build apk` all produce a bundle with the engine's entry points exported, and
the APK carries `arm64-v8a`, `armeabi-v7a` and `x86_64`.

The plugin is CocoaPods only. Swift Package Manager has no equivalent of
`prepare_command`, and the build has to run cargo before anything can be
vendored, so `flutter build` warns that this plugin does not support SPM. It
still builds; when SPM stops being optional, this is the thing that has to be
solved.

### 5. `LiraAudioEngine`

`lib/audio/lira_engine.dart`, implementing the same `AudioEngine`. The grid,
the sequencer, the exporter and their tests did not change, which was the point
of the interface from M0.

What came with it, as expected:

- **The audition paths moved.** SoLoud keeps one `AudioSource` per slice and
  per Kit slot, re-cut and re-encoded as WAV every time the division or the
  break changes, purely so a tap does not wait on the render queue. None of
  that is needed once the mixer is the callback: the audio is already there and
  a tap is one voice reading a range of it. That is `preview.rs`, and it is
  what Lira's `PreviewVoice` is.
- **`audio_session` stayed**, and moved to `lib/audio/platform_session.dart`
  so both engines claim the session the same way rather than one of them
  drifting.
- **The device decides the sample rate.** A phone runs its own clock, and a 48
  kHz device being asked for 44.1 and saying no is the normal case rather than
  a failure. `sampleRate` is read after `initialize` and everything the app
  decodes is resampled to it, which the studio already did in that order.

### 6. Both engines, behind a flag

`--dart-define=JE_LIRA_ENGINE=true`. The whole switch is one line in
`audioEngineProvider`, off by default.

### What it costs

- **The Rust toolchain is a build requirement.** No cargo, no build, on either
  platform, with an error that says so and links to rustup.
- **The device is open whenever the engine is.** Not only while playing: that
  is what makes a tapped pad sound at once instead of after a stream start.
  It is also a thing to watch in stage 3's battery leg. Stage 5 narrows it to
  whenever the app is in front of you: backgrounding hands the output back.
- **Everyone pays the build.** The plugin is a dependency whether or not the
  flag is on, so a clean build compiles the crate for every ABI either way.

## Stage 3: the A/B, on a phone

The gate is answered on a device, not on this desk.

- **Edit to audible.** Both engines now measure it and report it the same way,
  and the readout below puts it on screen. Then the same thing with a 240 fps
  camera on a thumb and a speaker, because the number an engine reports is the
  number it believes, and the gate is about what a person hears. Before:
  bounded by 0.24 s. After: expected one block.
- **Playhead accuracy.** Drift between the drawn playhead and the audible step,
  measured the same way.
- **Battery.** Thirty minutes of looping playback, screen on, same phone, same
  brightness, same break, from full. Twice per engine, alternating. Anything
  under a percentage point apart is a tie, and a tie is fine: the case is
  latency and the bench says the CPU difference is small.
- **Dropouts.** An hour of playback with the app backgrounded and foregrounded,
  a phone call taken, headphones pulled out. The current engine handles all of
  these through `audio_session`; the new one has to as well before it can ship.
  That is stage 5, and it is written: what is left here is the half a desk
  cannot do, which is taking an actual call.
- **The two things stage 2 could not answer here.** A real iPhone and a real
  Android device opening the output at all, and what happens on Android below
  API 26, where the shared object cannot load. That second one is answered in
  code now rather than left to the device: `LiraAudioEngine.isAvailable` asks
  the loader for the library before anything is built on it, and
  `audioEngineProvider` falls back to flutter_soloud with a line in the log
  when the answer is no. The flag is a request, the same way the sample rate
  is. A device whose loader takes the crate but whose *output* will not open is
  still the engine-failed screen, which is a legible failure rather than a
  quiet one, and is one of the things the phone is being asked about.

### The instrument, and what it counts

`AudioEngine.editLatency` is the measurement, on both engines, and
`--dart-define=JE_LATENCY_HUD=true` puts it on screen: median and worst of the
last 64 edits, the engine's name, and the rate it is running at. Tap it to
start the window again. It is untranslated and it is never in a build that did
not ask for it, for the same reason `JE_LOCALE` is debug only: it is an
instrument, not surface area, and it comes out when the gate is answered.

Both engines count the same stretch -- the edit being handed to the engine, to
the first sample of it being mixed -- and what is underneath that is the whole
argument:

- **flutter_soloud** counts the frames still queued ahead of the playhead at
  the moment of the edit, read from the device's own consumed count rather than
  assumed to be `_queueSeconds`. Everything already pushed carries the old
  pattern, so that queue *is* the wait.
- **The Lira engine** stamps the publication on the caller's thread and
  subtracts it from the clock in the callback that first renders it. A clock
  and not the callback's frame counter, which is the obvious thing and the
  wrong one: the counter only moves when a block is rendered, so an edit made
  between two callbacks and picked up by the next one would measure zero while
  a real fraction of a block went by.

Neither counts what happens after that, because that is where either engine's
knowledge stops: the platform's own buffer sits under both of them and neither
can see it. So the engine number is a floor, and the camera is what says
whether the floor is the whole story.

What the app itself costs is reported next to it rather than folded in: `CALL`
is what `setSpec` took on the UI thread, encoding the spec and, on the Lira
engine, copying it across the boundary. The first edit after a break loads
carries the upload with it and reads high; the median is the number.

To take the numbers, on the phone, with both engines at the hardware's rate --
which is whatever the Lira engine printed on open, 48000 below being the
example rather than the answer:

```
flutter run --release \
  --dart-define=JE_LIRA_ENGINE=true \
  --dart-define=JE_LATENCY_HUD=true \
  --dart-define=JE_SAMPLE_RATE=48000
```

then the same line without `JE_LIRA_ENGINE`. Play a Beat, paint thirty or forty
steps at an ordinary pace, and read the median and the worst off the readout.
Release, not debug: a debug build measures the Dart VM as much as the engine,
and the readout is deliberately not behind `kDebugMode` for exactly that
reason.

### The sonic difference, which was not predicted

First listen on an iPhone 16e: the Lira engine sounded clearly better, not just
tighter. That should be impossible. The two mixers agree sample for sample on
eight fixtures across both the playback and the export path, so whatever the
difference is, it is downstream of the mixer and belongs to how the samples
reach the speaker.

Both engines now print the rate they are running at, because that is what
separates the candidates:

- **The Lira engine prints the rate the device actually gave it.** cpal on iOS
  *reads* `AVAudioSession.sampleRate` rather than asking for one, so this is
  the hardware's rate. Every bundled asset is 44100.
- **SoLoud prints the rate it was initialised at**, which is all it knows. It
  deliberately leaves the platform session alone, so on a device running at
  something else it is converting its output continuously, on every sample.

Two readings, and they point in opposite directions:

- **If the device reports 48000**, the Lira engine won *despite* being the one
  doing the cruder conversion: `AudioClip.resampledTo` **was** linear
  interpolation, documented as not being on the critical path because it only
  ever ran on the odd mismatched asset. Under the Lira engine at a non-44.1
  rate it runs on every break and every one shot. That makes the feed path the
  suspect -- SoLoud's buffer stream is topped up from a 12 ms Dart timer with
  0.08 s of slack, and SoLoud pauses a stream that gets too close to the end of
  its buffer, which on a busy UI thread is intermittent grit. It also meant the
  Lira engine had room to get *better* by replacing that resampler, which is
  stage 4 and is done.
- **If the device reports 44100**, neither engine is resampling anything and
  the feed path is the only difference left, which is the same answer arrived
  at more directly.

Either way this is evidence for the structural argument rather than against it.
But it is not the latency argument the gate was built on, so it does not get to
stand in for it: `--dart-define=JE_SAMPLE_RATE=<device rate>` runs both engines
at the hardware's rate so resampling stops being a difference between them, and
the A/B below is the thing that answers the gate.

Then answer the gate in one line, in this file, with the numbers next to it.

## Stage 4: the resampler (done)

The one thing above that could be fixed at the desk rather than on a phone, and
the only place the Lira engine was measurably *worse* than the one it wants to
replace.

`AudioClip.resampledTo` was linear interpolation, with a comment saying quality
was not on the critical path because it only ever ran on an asset that was not
already at 44100. That was true right up until an engine that takes the
hardware's rate instead of asking for one. On a 48 kHz phone the Lira engine
puts every bundled break, every one shot and every import through it.

Linear interpolation between two samples is a triangular filter, which is about
6 dB of rejection at Nyquist. Going up it leaves the images in, and going down
it folds everything above the new Nyquist straight back into the music. On a
break, which is mostly cymbals and snare transients, that is precisely the
material it damages most.

`lib/audio/resample.dart` replaces it with the ordinary answer: a Kaiser
windowed sinc, 32 zero crossings either side, beta 9, read per output frame
from one table built once and shared by every clip. Flat to 20 kHz at 44100,
and the roll off happens in the octave above that, where there is nothing to
lose. `tool/resample_bench.dart` puts a tone through both and reads off what
came out, AOT, on the same M4 Pro as the mixer bench:

| | linear | sinc |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 kHz, 44.1 -> 48, worst sample error | 2.53e-3 | 8.94e-6 |
| 15 kHz, 44.1 -> 48, level of the tone | -3.4 dB | -0.0 dB |
| 15 kHz, 44.1 -> 22.05, level of the 7050 Hz fold | 0.0 dB | -103.9 dB |
| 5.65 s stereo break, 44.1 -> 48 | 1.0 ms | 35.4 ms |

The third row is the one to look at. Downsampling a 15 kHz tone with linear
interpolation puts the alias back at **full level**: not attenuated, moved.
The second row is the audible half of the same thing going the other way -- 3.4
dB off the top of the band, on every hat in the break, on a phone running at
48 kHz.

The cost is thirty five times what it was and it is still nothing next to the
audio thread, because it is nowhere near it. Clips convert once, when they
load. What that is worth avoiding on the UI isolate is a different question:
35 ms for a bundled break on this desk is a few times that on a phone, and an
import can be three minutes of audio, which is about a second of filtering. So
both go through `resampledToOffThread`, which spawns nothing when the rates
already agree -- which is every bundled asset on a device that runs at 44100,
and every widget test, whose fake clock does not pump a real isolate's reply.

`test/audio/resample_test.dart` is the guard: DC comes out at the level it went
in, a tone reproduces to under 5e-4, the top of the band survives going up,
nothing folds back going down, the channels do not leak into each other, and
the isolate returns exactly what the main one would have.

This changes nothing about the gate. It is not a latency argument and it does
not belong in the A/B table: both engines share `AudioClip`, so both got the
better converter at the same moment and the difference between them is
untouched. What it does is remove a confound -- the reading above where the
Lira engine won *despite* doing the cruder conversion no longer has a cruder
conversion in it -- and take away the one honest objection to shipping the Rust
engine on a device that does not run at 44100.

## Stage 5: the interruption (done, except on a phone)

The other half of what stage 3 asks for, and the last thing on that list a desk
can answer. Its dropout leg is not a measurement, it is a condition: *the
current engine handles all of these through `audio_session`; the new one has to
as well before it can ship.* It did not. `device.rs` opened one stream and held
it for the life of the engine, and its error callback printed a line.

A desktop DAW can hold an output for as long as it is running. A phone cannot,
and the list of things that take it away is not exotic:

- a call, an alarm, another app claiming the session
- headphones pulled out, which reroutes rather than removes
- the app going to the background, where nothing can keep a stream fed anyway
- media services restarting, which invalidates every audio object in the process

Before this, the first and the last of those left a dead `AudioUnit` and no
sound until the app was restarted, and the third left an open device with
nobody listening -- which is the same battery leg stage 3 is told to watch.

### Giving the device back

`Engine::suspend` closes the stream; `Engine::resume` opens it again. Both are
messages to the control thread, because it is the only thread allowed to touch
a `cpal::Stream`, and both are answered rather than fired: the caller is a
lifecycle callback and the platform expects it to have finished.

Closing is the interesting half. cpal takes the data callback by value and
frees it with the stream, and that callback owns everything the mixer *is*: the
renderer, the plan that is loaded, the consumer ends of both rings. None of it
can be rebuilt from the control thread -- the plan was published, not kept, and
a ring consumer has no second end. So it travels in a `SideCarrier` that hands
it back over a channel as the closure is freed, and the next stream carries on
with it. A suspend and a resume are a stream teardown, not a reload: the break
is still uploaded, the pattern is still installed, the tap that follows still
sounds at once.

The transport stops with it, and the stop is *queued into the command ring*
rather than done on the control thread, because `playing` belongs to the
callback and only the callback may move it. It lands on the first block after
the device comes back, before a sample of it is rendered. A transport that was
running when the output went away is not running when it returns, which is the
behaviour anyone would expect after a phone call.

### Knowing the device went away

`TransportShared` grew one field, `device_state`, and it is the one field there
the audio callback does not write, so it sits outside the seqlock: cpal's error
callback stores it, the control thread stores it, Dart reads it off the same
mapped pointer it already reads the playhead from. No call, and nothing
mirrored on the Dart side that could disagree with it.

The error callback triages rather than panics, which cpal 0.18 makes possible
by giving errors kinds. `DeviceChanged` is a stream that was rerouted and is
still playing, `Xrun` is a glitch that has already happened, `RealtimeDenied`
is a warning about the future: tearing a stream down for any of those would
turn a click into a silence. Everything else means the stream has to be rebuilt,
which is not something a callback may do, so it says so and the control thread
closes what is left on its next tick.

It then leaves it closed. Reopening from there would be reopening into whatever
took the output -- a call that has not ended -- so it fails, and it would fail
in a loop. The device comes back when something says the output is available,
and on a phone that something is the session.

### What the app does with all this

`configureAudioSession` now takes three callbacks rather than one, and the
sizes are deliberately different:

- **headphones out**, or a duck beginning: stop. The output is still ours and
  the route has simply moved to the speaker. Handing the device back for that
  would be a teardown for a route change. Stopping rather than ducking, because
  there is no such thing as a quiet sequencer step.
- **an interruption beginning**: suspend. The output is gone whether or not the
  app agrees.
- **that interruption ending**: claim the session, then resume. Both allowed to
  fail, because whatever interrupted us may still hold the session, and the
  next thing that says otherwise will try again.

Playback never restarts by itself. A call ending is not a request to play.

`AppLifecycleState.paused` suspends and `resumed` resumes, next to the save
that was already there. And `start` is the one place a closed output is
reopened without being told to, because a thumb on the transport is a person
saying they expect sound now.

Two smaller things fall out of the output being closed while the app is still
on screen, which is what a phone call is:

- **Edits are held, not published.** Painting carries on during a call. The
  plan ring is sized for a thumb against a running callback, and thirty two
  edits into a device nobody is draining would start pushing the newest out.
  The spec that is on screen when the output returns is the one it gets.
- **Taps go nowhere.** A tapped slice is feedback for a finger that has already
  moved on, and hearing a fistful of them at once when the call ends is worse
  than hearing none.

### Coming back at another rate

A phone can change route while the output is closed and come back on another
clock: headphones in during a call, the speaker after one. Everything the app
has decoded is then at the old rate and would play at the wrong speed.

So `resume` returns the rate the way `initialize` does, and when it has moved
the engine says so through `AudioEngine.onSampleRateChanged`. It cannot fix it
itself: it holds samples, and the bundled asset or imported file they came from
is the studio's business. The studio decodes the project's audio again -- the
same load the boot does, through the same band limited converter stage 4 put
there -- and republishes. The transport is stopped throughout, because a resume
does not start playback.

Rare, and deliberately not clever. The alternative is resampling inside the
engine, which would put a converter on the audio thread to handle a case that
happens when somebody plugs in headphones during a phone call.

### What flutter_soloud does instead

`SoLoudAudioEngine.suspend` stops, and that is all it does, and the comment on
it says why: SoLoud owns its backend device from `init` to `deinit`, and a
`deinit` would take every cached `AudioSource` with it -- every slice, every
Kit slot -- to be re-cut and re-encoded on the way back. Tearing that down for
a phone call is a worse trade than leaving an idle device open, and this is the
engine that is not being changed while it is the one that ships.

So the two engines are honestly different here, and the difference has a
direction: the Rust engine gives a backgrounded phone its output back and the
current one does not. That belongs in the battery leg of the A/B rather than
hidden behind a method that pretends they do the same thing. It is not a
latency argument and it does not go in the table.

### What is proved, and what is not

`cargo run --bin je_device` now suspends and resumes in the middle of its run:
the device closes, a second passes, it opens again and the same plan keeps
rendering -- 88064 frames in the two seconds after, which is exactly two
seconds of 44100. `test/audio/lira_engine_test.dart` drives the same thing
through `AudioEngine`: the transport stops with the device, an edit made while
it is closed is what plays when it opens, play reopens a suspended output, and
shutdown while suspended is clean. `test/features/lifecycle_test.dart` puts the
app through backgrounding and foregrounding, and `test/state/studio_test.dart`
brings the output back at 48 kHz and watches the project's audio get decoded
again.

None of that is a phone call. What a desk cannot do is take one, pull the
headphones out of a real device, or leave the app in the background for half an
hour and see what the battery says -- and those are stage 3's, on the same
phone, in the same sitting as the numbers.

### One thing found on the way, which matters more than stage 5 does

The pod's script phase was not running. It declared an output file the build
script never writes, and Xcode reads that as a phase it has already done, so
after the first build the crate was only ever rebuilt by hand. The macOS
podspec also passed `ios` to the script, so even a hand run built the wrong
platform. Both are fixed -- `:always_out_of_date`, and the right argument --
and both are the sort of bug that does not announce itself: the build works,
the app runs, and what is in it is last week's engine.

Which would have been found in a measurement, on a phone, with a stale engine
in the build and no reason to suspect one. Verified afterwards on both: a
touched Rust file rebuilds the vendored library, and `nm -g` on the framework
inside the built app finds the twenty entry points including the two stage 5
added. With the caveat that is now in the plugin's README -- an Apple build
picks the vendored artifact before the phase runs, so a Rust edit takes two
builds to reach the app.

## The honest position today

Stage 2 cost less than it might have: cpal's phone backends worked, Android
needs no JVM wiring, and the interface written at M0 held -- the grid, the
sequencer, the exporter and their tests did not move a line. Stage 3 has built
the instrument the gate is answered with and the fallback that keeps a phone
that cannot load the crate making noise, stage 4 has taken away the one thing
that made the Rust engine measurably worse than flutter_soloud on a phone that
does not run at 44100, and stage 5 has made it survive the things a phone does
to an app that is holding its output. What none of it has done is take a number
on a phone. Everything above was proved on a desktop, and a desktop is
not where the argument is. On this one, an adopted edit reaches the callback in
about 0.6 ms against a 512 frame block, which says the measurement is real and
nothing more: a Mac is not a phone and a test run is not a thumb.

So the position is unchanged. If the phone A/B shows edits landing in 30 ms
instead of 240 ms, that is a real improvement to a real interaction and the
merge is justified. If it shows something closer to 100 ms because of a
platform buffer nobody controls, the honest answer is to keep flutter_soloud,
lower `_queueSeconds` as far as it will go without underrunning, and spend the
time on content packs. MILESTONES.md says as much, and nothing in stages 2
through 5 changes it: an engine that works, converts cleanly and survives a
phone call is still not evidence that it is a better one.

Everything left is on a device. Stage 3's list is the whole of it.
